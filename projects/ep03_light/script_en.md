# How Do We Know the Speed of Light?

Target: ~9 min narration (~1,350 words at ~150 wpm).
Format per block: **VO** → *VISUAL*.

Legend: `PX` Pexels · `NASA` NASA imagery · `3D` our Blender render · `GFX` motion graphics

---

## 0. Cold open (0:00–0:50)

**VO:** The speed of light is 299,792,458 metres per second. Not approximately. Exactly.
*VISUAL: GFX — the number, sharp, on black*

**VO:** Sunlight takes about eight minutes to reach us. Everyone knows that. But the number behind it is strange. It has no error bar. No last digit still being argued over. And every other measurement ever made has one.
*VISUAL: Earth from orbit with the sun at the rim; brass ruler; oscilloscope*

**VO:** The reason is that we stopped measuring it. Today the speed of light is a decision — and everything else bends around it.
*VISUAL: GFX — the number locking into place*

**VO:** Getting there took three hundred years, a moon of Jupiter, a spinning wheel with 720 teeth, a mountain in California, and a laser that finally made the question meaningless.
*VISUAL: quick cuts — Jupiter (3D), toothed wheel (3D), mountain (PX), laser (3D)*

**VO:** This is how we learned how fast light is.
*VISUAL: title card*

## I. The moon that ran late (0:50–3:00)

**VO:** For most of history, the honest answer was that light had no speed at all. Galileo tried to measure it with two lanterns on two hills, and found only that light was faster than two men with shutters.
*VISUAL: lantern in the dark, hills at dusk (PX)*

**VO:** The first real answer came from the wrong problem. In the 1670s, astronomers in Paris were building tables for the moons of Jupiter — because a moon that eclipses on schedule is a clock that everyone on Earth can see, and a clock like that solves navigation.
*VISUAL: 3D — Jupiter with Io approaching the shadow; old star chart (PX)*

**VO:** Io, the innermost large moon, goes behind Jupiter every forty-two and a half hours. Predictable. Except it wasn't. The eclipses ran early when Earth was closest to Jupiter, and late when Earth was farthest away.
*VISUAL: 3D — Io entering Jupiter's shadow, timer drifting*

**VO:** In 1676, the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer said the obvious thing that nobody wanted to say: the eclipses are on time. The light is late.
*VISUAL: GFX — Earth's orbit, two positions, light crossing*

**VO:** When Earth is on the far side of its orbit, the light from Io has to cross the extra width of that orbit to reach us. Rømer estimated that crossing took about twenty-two minutes.
*VISUAL: 3D/GFX — the extra distance drawn across the orbit*

**VO:** With the orbit sizes we know today, twenty-two minutes gives a speed of light of about 227,000 kilometres per second. That is about twenty-five per cent too low.
*VISUAL: GFX — 227,000 km/s vs 299,792 km/s*

**VO:** And it did not matter. Rømer's number was wrong; Rømer's point was right. Light takes time. After 1676, the speed of light was a quantity — something with a value that could be measured better.
*VISUAL: 3D — Jupiter, slow drift, stars behind*

---

## II. A wheel with 720 teeth (3:00–5:00)

**VO:** The next step took another 170 years, and it happened on the ground.
*VISUAL: Paris rooftops (PX)*

**VO:** In 1849, Hippolyte Fizeau sent a beam of light from a house in Suresnes to a mirror on Montmartre — a round trip of about seventeen kilometres — and put a spinning toothed wheel in its path.
*VISUAL: 3D — the toothed wheel spinning, beam passing between teeth*

**VO:** Light goes out through a gap between two teeth, travels to the mirror, and comes back. If the wheel has turned by exactly one tooth while the light was away, the returning beam hits metal, and the observer sees nothing.
*VISUAL: 3D — close on the teeth, beam blocked*

**VO:** Fizeau turned the wheel faster and faster until the light went out. Seven hundred and twenty teeth, twenty-five turns per second, seventeen kilometres of travel — and the speed of light fell out of the arithmetic: 313,000 kilometres per second.
*VISUAL: GFX — 720 teeth · 25.2 rev/s · 8,633 m*

**VO:** About four and a half per cent too high, measured with a wheel, a lamp and a mirror on a hill.
*VISUAL: 3D — wide shot of the apparatus in the dark*

**VO:** Thirteen years later, Léon Foucault replaced the wheel with a rotating mirror, which let him do the whole experiment on a bench instead of across a city — with a light path of only about twenty metres. In 1862 he got 298,000 kilometres per second, within about six tenths of a per cent of the modern value.
*VISUAL: 3D — rotating mirror, beam deflected by a small angle*

**VO:** For the first time, the number was right to within a per cent. The argument moved to the next digit.
*VISUAL: GFX — digits filling in one by one*

---

## III. Two mountains (5:00–6:30)

**VO:** In 1926, Albert Michelson ran the experiment across the sky of southern California — from Mount Wilson to Mount San Antonio, a distance of about thirty-five kilometres, surveyed to within a few centimetres.
*VISUAL: mountains, California ridges at dawn (PX)*

**VO:** At one end, an eight-sided mirror spun at five hundred and twenty-eight turns a second. Light left one face, crossed the valley, came back, and struck the next face — but only if the mirror had turned exactly one eighth of a revolution while the light was in the air.
*VISUAL: 3D — the octagonal mirror spinning, beam leaving and returning*

**VO:** His answer: 299,796 kilometres per second, give or take one.
*VISUAL: GFX — 299,796 ± 1 km/s*

**VO:** By now the limit was no longer the cleverness of the experiment. It was the ruler. To state a speed you need a distance, and the metre itself was a scratch on a bar of metal in a vault in Paris.
*VISUAL: metal bar macro, vault (PX); callback to the kilogram cylinder (3D from EP02)*

---

## IV. The laser that ended the question (6:30–8:00)

**VO:** Speed is distance over time. By the 1960s, time had become extraordinarily good: atomic clocks could count the seconds to eleven digits. Distance could not keep up.
*VISUAL: lab equipment, atomic clock (PX / NIST)*

**VO:** Then came the laser. A stabilised laser has a frequency you can count and a wavelength you can measure. Multiply them together and you get a speed — directly, in one measurement.
*VISUAL: 3D — laser beam, wave overlay; GFX c = λ × f*

**VO:** In 1972, at the National Bureau of Standards in Boulder, a team led by Kenneth Evenson did exactly that with a methane-stabilised laser. Their result: 299,792,456.2 metres per second, with an uncertainty of 1.1 metres per second — a hundred times better than anything before it.
*VISUAL: GFX — the number with its error bar; NIST-style lab (PX)*

**VO:** And that created a problem. The measurement was now more precise than the definition of the metre it was measured against. The uncertainty in the speed of light was, almost entirely, the uncertainty in the ruler.
*VISUAL: GFX — the error bar shrinking to the ruler*

---

## V. The decision (8:00–9:00)

**VO:** In 1983, the General Conference on Weights and Measures took the obvious way out. It stopped measuring the speed of light and defined it: exactly 299,792,458 metres per second.
*VISUAL: GFX — the number locking; conference hall (PX)*

**VO:** Then it redefined the metre in terms of that number. A metre is the distance light travels in one 299-million-792-thousand-458th of a second.
*VISUAL: 3D — light travelling a marked distance*

**VO:** The trade was deliberate. We gave up the ability to measure the speed of light, and in exchange we got a ruler that is the same everywhere in the universe, forever.
*VISUAL: 3D — beam crossing space; Earth from orbit (NASA)*

**VO:** It is the same move that would be made for the kilogram thirty-six years later: replace an object with a constant, and let the object become just an object.
*VISUAL: 3D — cylinder under bell jars (EP02 callback)*

**VO:** So when someone asks how we know the speed of light, the honest answer has two parts. We measured it for three hundred years, from a late moon to a spinning wheel to a laser. And then we stopped, because we had learned enough to make it a definition.
*VISUAL: 3D — Jupiter and Io, final slow drift*

**VO:** The last digit stopped moving in 1983.
*VISUAL: end card*
