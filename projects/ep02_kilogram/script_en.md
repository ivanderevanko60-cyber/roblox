# The Kilogram Was Losing Weight

Target: ~9 min narration (~1,350 words at ~150 wpm).
Format per block: **VO** → *VISUAL* (source).

Legend: `PX` Pexels · `NIST` NIST/BIPM media · `3D` our Blender render · `GFX` motion graphics

## 0. Cold open (0:00–0:50)

**VO:** The most important lump of metal in the world has been losing weight. Or everything else has been gaining it. For a hundred and thirty years, nobody could tell which.
*VISUAL: 3D — the Pt-Ir cylinder under nested bell jars, slow push-in*

**VO:** Because that lump of metal was the kilogram. Not a copy of it — the unit itself. By definition it weighed exactly one kilogram, and it could never be wrong.
*VISUAL: 3D — reflection of the cylinder in the glass; GFX "1.000 000 000 kg"*

**VO:** And at some point in the twentieth century, it started to disagree with its own copies.
*VISUAL: 3D — two identical cylinders side by side, one tilting a balance*

**VO:** Not by much. By about the weight of a fingerprint. But when the object in the vault defines the unit, a disagreement that small is a problem with no solution — because the cylinder cannot be wrong.
*VISUAL: macro of a fingerprint on glass (PX)*

**VO:** This is the story of how we noticed, and what we had to do about it.
*VISUAL: title card*

## I. The metal (0:50–3:00)

**VO:** The kilogram began as a decision. In 1889, the first General Conference on Weights and Measures sanctioned a cylinder of platinum and iridium — ninety per cent platinum, ten per cent iridium — about thirty-nine millimetres tall and thirty-nine across.
*VISUAL: 3D — cylinder rotating slowly on black; GFX "90% Pt · 10% Ir · 39 mm"*

**VO:** The alloy was chosen for a reason. Platinum is dense, so the object is small and its surface area is tiny. It doesn't rust, doesn't oxidise, and barely reacts with anything. Iridium makes it harder, so it wears less when handled.
*VISUAL: macro of polished metal, reflections (PX)*

**VO:** It was given a name that stuck: Le Grand K. It lived in a safe at the International Bureau of Weights and Measures, in Sèvres, under three bell jars, one inside the other. Three keys, held by three different people, were needed to reach it.
*VISUAL: vault door, keys, Paris exterior (PX)*

**VO:** Copies were made from the same casting and distributed to countries, so that every nation could compare its own standards with something traceable back to the vault.
*VISUAL: 3D — a row of identical cylinders under glass*

**VO:** And here is the part that sounds like a joke: in its entire working life, the official prototype was taken out and compared with its copies only a handful of times. Roughly once every forty years.
*VISUAL: GFX timeline 1889 · 1946–48 · 1988–92*

**VO:** The reason was simple. Every comparison meant handling, cleaning and exposure to air. Every measurement risked changing the thing being measured.
*VISUAL: gloved hands lifting a polished object (PX)*

---

## II. The drift (3:00–5:00)

**VO:** The third periodic verification ran from 1988 to 1992. The prototype came out of the safe, and the copies were brought back to Sèvres from around the world.
*VISUAL: 3D — bell jars lifted; lab bench with balance*

**VO:** When everything was weighed against everything else, the numbers didn't line up. Over roughly a century, the copies had gained mass relative to the prototype — on average by about fifty micrograms.
*VISUAL: GFX +50 µg drift chart rising*

**VO:** Fifty micrograms is fifty millionths of a gram. It is less than a grain of sand, less than a speck of dust you would never see.
*VISUAL: macro sand grains / dust in a light beam (PX)*

**VO:** And nobody could say what had actually happened. Maybe the copies were gaining mass from contamination. Maybe the prototype was losing mass, shedding material from its surface every time it was cleaned. Most likely, both.
*VISUAL: 3D — surface of metal, particles settling*

**VO:** But the logic of the old definition made the question impossible to answer. The prototype defined the kilogram, so its mass was one kilogram, always — by definition, with zero drift. If it changed, the kilogram changed with it, and the rest of the world quietly changed underneath.
*VISUAL: GFX — the ruler that stretches while measuring itself*

**VO:** And the kilogram was not alone. Under the old system it propped up other units: the mole, the ampere, the candela all carried its definition inside them. A drifting cylinder in a French vault was a foundation for a sizeable part of the measurement system.
*VISUAL: GFX — units tree, kilogram at the root*

**VO:** So the plan changed. Instead of a better object, physics would use something that cannot be scratched, contaminated or dropped: a constant of nature.
*VISUAL: 3D — cylinder fading into a field of light*

---

## III. Weighing with electricity (5:00–7:00)

**VO:** The first route was a machine called the Kibble balance, invented by Bryan Kibble in Britain in 1975.
*VISUAL: NIST photo of a Kibble balance; PX lab*

**VO:** It works in two modes. In the first, a mass sits on a pan attached to a coil inside a magnetic field. Current flows through the coil until the magnetic force exactly cancels the weight of the mass.
*VISUAL: 3D — coil in a magnet gap, balance beam settling*

**VO:** In the second, the mass is taken away, and the same coil is moved through the same field at a measured speed, generating a voltage.
*VISUAL: 3D — coil sliding, laser beam measuring velocity*

**VO:** Put the two together, and the strength of the magnet — the hardest thing in the machine to measure — cancels out. What remains links a mass to electrical measurements: to voltage, to resistance, and through them to the Planck constant.
*VISUAL: GFX equation m = IV / gv, terms cancelling*

**VO:** The American version, NIST-4, stands two and a half metres tall in a room underground and works in vacuum, because even the buoyancy of air is a disturbance at this level.
*VISUAL: NIST photo of NIST-4; PX underground corridor*

**VO:** Between 2015 and 2017 it weighed masses more than ten thousand times. The result was a value for the Planck constant with an uncertainty of thirteen parts per billion.
*VISUAL: GFX 6.626 069 934 × 10⁻³⁴ · 13 ppb*

**VO:** Thirteen parts per billion is roughly one part in eighty million — the difference of a spoonful of water in an Olympic swimming pool.
*VISUAL: still water surface, drop falling (PX)*

---

## IV. Counting atoms (7:00–8:30)

**VO:** The second route took the opposite approach. If a kilogram is an amount of matter, then count the matter.
*VISUAL: 3D — the silicon sphere, mirror-polished, rotating*

**VO:** The Avogadro project grew crystals of silicon-28 — one isotope, purified to 99.9995 per cent — and ground them into spheres of almost exactly one kilogram, just under ninety-four millimetres across.
*VISUAL: 3D — sphere with a ruler-like caliper; GFX "93.6 mm · 99.9995% ²⁸Si"*

**VO:** They are among the roundest objects ever made. The surface deviates from a perfect sphere by about fifty nanometres. Scaled up to the size of the Earth, the distance from the highest mountain to the deepest trench would be a few metres.
*VISUAL: 3D — sphere reflecting a lab; GFX Earth comparison*

**VO:** Because the crystal is pure and its lattice spacing is known, measuring the sphere's diameter tells you how many atoms are inside it — a number with twenty-odd digits, known well enough to matter.
*VISUAL: 3D — lattice of atoms inside the sphere*

**VO:** Two completely different methods, one counting atoms and one balancing electricity against gravity, had to agree. They did.
*VISUAL: GFX two values converging*

---

## V. The vote (8:30–9:15)

**VO:** On the sixteenth of November 2018, in Versailles, the General Conference on Weights and Measures voted.
*VISUAL: PX conference hall, hands raised*

**VO:** The Planck constant was fixed, by agreement, at exactly 6.62607015 times ten to the minus thirty-four joule seconds. No uncertainty, no further measurement — a defined value, like the speed of light.
*VISUAL: GFX the number, locked*

**VO:** On the twentieth of May 2019, the new definition took effect. The kilogram stopped being a thing and became a consequence.
*VISUAL: 3D — cylinder under glass, lights going out*

**VO:** Le Grand K is still in its vault, still under its bell jars. But it is now just an object. Its mass is measured like anything else, and it is allowed to drift.
*VISUAL: 3D — final slow orbit around the cylinder*

**VO:** For a hundred and thirty years, humanity kept its unit of mass in a safe, and had no way to ask whether it was still right. Now the question can be asked anywhere, by anyone with the right machine — and the answer will be the same in a thousand years.
*VISUAL: 3D — sphere and cylinder side by side, fading to black*

**VO:** The last digit finally stopped moving.
*VISUAL: end card*
