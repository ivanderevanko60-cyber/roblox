# Nobody Knows What Time It Is

Target: ~7.5 min narration. Lines kept short: one idea each, so the narration can breathe and the cut can move.
Format per block: **VO** → *VISUAL*.

---

## 0. Cold open (0:00–0:45)

**VO(confident):** On the thirtieth of June 2012, the world added one second to the clock.
*VISUAL: a digital clock rolling 23:59:59 → 23:59:60*

**VO(excited):** Within minutes, Reddit went down. An airline in Australia couldn't check in its passengers. Servers across the internet froze.
*VISUAL: server racks, error screens, airport departures board — fast*

**VO(confident):** All because of a second that, on most clocks, isn't even allowed to exist.
*VISUAL: clock face, second hand*

**VO(confident):** That second exists because of something most people never hear: right now, nobody on Earth knows exactly what time it is.
*VISUAL: city at night, phone lock screen*

**VO:** Not your phone. Not the satellites overhead. The official time for this moment will be worked out weeks from now, in an office outside Paris.
*VISUAL: satellites, Paris*

**VO(confident):** This is the story of who decides what time it is — and why we stopped trusting the Earth to do it.
*VISUAL: Earth from orbit; title over footage*

---

## I. Every town had its own noon (0:45–2:10)

**VO:** For most of history, time came from the Sun. When it was highest in the sky, it was noon.
*VISUAL: sundial, sunrise timelapse, shadows moving*

**VO:** Which meant every town had its own noon. In Bristol, it came about ten minutes after London's.
*VISUAL: old town clock, street*

**VO(cheerful):** The clock on Bristol's Corn Exchange still has two minute hands. One for Bristol. One for London.
*VISUAL: close-up of an old clock face*

**VO:** Then came the railways. A timetable with a different time at every station was a recipe for collisions. In 1880, Britain made Greenwich time the law.
*VISUAL: steam train, station clock*

**VO:** And a second was still just a slice of a day: one eighty-six-thousand-four-hundredth of a turn of the Earth.
*VISUAL: Earth rotating*

**VO(confident):** There was only one problem. The Earth is a terrible clock.
*VISUAL: Earth, slow push in*

**VO:** The tides drag on it like a brake. Earthquakes, melting ice and the churning core nudge it faster, then slower. No two days are exactly the same length.
*VISUAL: ocean tides, glaciers, lava*

---

## II. The atom that never gets tired (2:10–3:30)

**VO:** So in the 1950s, scientists stopped looking up at the sky — and looked inside an atom instead.
*VISUAL: night sky → glowing lab equipment*

**VO:** A caesium atom absorbs microwaves at one exact frequency. Every caesium atom in the universe has the same one. It never wears out. It never slows down.
*VISUAL: lab laser, particles*

**VO(confident):** In 1955, near London, Louis Essen built the first clock that counted those microwaves. And it showed something awkward almost immediately.
*VISUAL: vintage lab, old instruments*

**VO(confident):** The atom was keeping perfect time. It was the planet that was wobbling.
*VISUAL: Earth, clock hands*

**VO:** Astronomers had kept the world's time for centuries. They did not give it up easily. It took twelve years.
*VISUAL: observatory dome, telescope*

**VO:** In 1967, the second was redefined: nine billion, one hundred and ninety-two million, six hundred and thirty-one thousand, seven hundred and seventy vibrations of caesium. Exactly.
*VISUAL: GFX — 9 192 631 770*

---

## III. The second that breaks computers (3:30–5:00)

**VO:** That solved one problem and created another. Atomic time is perfect. Earth time is not. And people still want noon to happen when the Sun is high.
*VISUAL: sun at noon, city at midday*

**VO:** So since 1972, whenever the planet falls almost a second behind the atoms, the world adds one. 23:59:59. Then 23:59:60. Then midnight.
*VISUAL: GFX — 23:59:60*

**VO(confident):** It has happened twenty-seven times. And the one in 2012 is the one that took down Reddit.
*VISUAL: server racks, code on screens*

**VO:** Software assumes a minute has sixty seconds. Give it sixty-one, and some of it simply stops.
*VISUAL: data centre corridor, blinking lights*

**VO(cheerful):** Google stopped playing along. Now it smears the extra second across a whole day, running its clocks very slightly slow until the second has quietly disappeared.
*VISUAL: data centre, servers*

**VO:** In 2022, the world gave up. The leap second will be abolished by 2035, and atomic time and the Sun will be allowed to drift apart.
*VISUAL: conference hall, Earth rotating*

**VO(excited):** But there's a twist. Lately, the Earth has been spinning faster. For the first time in history, the next leap second might have to be taken away.
*VISUAL: Earth timelapse, spinning*

---

## IV. Four hundred clocks vote (5:00–6:15)

**VO:** So who actually decides what time it is? Not one clock. Even atomic clocks drift, each in its own way.
*VISUAL: many clocks, close-ups of hands ticking*

**VO:** Instead, more than four hundred atomic clocks in about eighty laboratories send their readings to the International Bureau of Weights and Measures, just outside Paris.
*VISUAL: world map, labs, Paris*

**VO:** There they are compared and averaged. The steadiest clocks get the biggest vote. The result is Coordinated Universal Time — the time the whole world runs on.
*VISUAL: GFX — clocks merging into one line*

**VO(confident):** And here is the strange part. The answer is published once a month — for the month that has already gone.
*VISUAL: calendar, papers*

**VO:** Official time only ever exists in the past. Every clock you own is showing a prediction of what that answer will turn out to be. Usually right to within a few billionths of a second.
*VISUAL: clock faces, phone screen*

---

## V. Clocks in the sky (6:15–7:15)

**VO:** Your phone gets its time from satellites, twenty thousand kilometres up, each carrying atomic clocks.
*VISUAL: satellite in orbit, phone map*

**VO:** Up there, time runs differently. Weaker gravity makes their clocks run fast. Their speed makes them run slow. Add it up, and each one gains thirty-eight millionths of a second every day.
*VISUAL: Earth from orbit, GFX +38 µs / day*

**VO(excited):** Leave that uncorrected, and your map would be wrong by around ten kilometres — by tomorrow.
*VISUAL: car navigation, city from above*

**VO:** So the satellite clocks are built to tick slightly slow on the ground. In orbit, Einstein makes them right.
*VISUAL: rocket launch, satellite*

---

## VI. The last digit (7:15–8:00)

**VO:** The newest clocks use light instead of microwaves. Lift one by a single centimetre, and it measurably ticks faster.
*VISUAL: laser lab, optical table*

**VO:** Time isn't even the same from floor to floor. Walk upstairs, and your head ages faster than your feet — we made a whole video about that.
*VISUAL: staircase, a person climbing*

**VO(confident):** So what time is it, right now? The honest answer is: we'll know next month. Until then, four hundred clocks are voting.
*VISUAL: city at dusk, clocks*

**VO:** The last digit of time is always a little bit in the future.
*VISUAL: end card*
