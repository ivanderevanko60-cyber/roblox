**VO:** Right now, your head is older than your feet.
*VISUAL: slow-motion close-up of a person standing, camera tilts from shoes up to face (PX)*

*VISUAL: macro of a watch second hand (PX) → freeze*

*VISUAL: 3D atom cloud in the glass cell, slow push-in (3D — atom_cell)*

**VO:** We raced particles down a mountain. We bought plane tickets for clocks. We lifted a clock by the height of two stair steps… and then we did something even more absurd.

**VO:** Four experiments. Each one smaller than the last. And by the end, time won't be the thing you thought it was.

## 1. The Mountain (0:45–3:15)

**VO:** Every second, particles from deep space slam into the top of our atmosphere. The collisions set off showers of debris — and some of that debris is a particle called the muon.
*VISUAL: Earth from orbit, atmosphere limb (NASA) → 3D/GFX cosmic-ray shower*

**VO:** A muon is like a heavier cousin of the electron, with one big problem: it doesn't last. On average, a muon falls apart in about two millionths of a second.
*VISUAL: GFX particle flickering out; timer 0.0000022 s*

**VO:** And muons are born high up — many kilometers above our heads. Even moving at nearly the speed of light, most of them should decay long before they reach the ground.
*VISUAL: aerial clouds from above, descending through (PX)*

**VO:** And yet, detectors at sea level catch them all the time.
*VISUAL: city street at sea level, people walking (PX)*

**VO:** In the early 1960s, two physicists, David Frisch and James Smith, decided to count them properly. They hauled a detector to the summit of Mount Washington in New Hampshire — almost two kilometers up — and counted muons moving at one specific speed.
*VISUAL: mountain summit, weather, observatory-like buildings (PX/PB); caption "Mount Washington, New Hampshire"*

**VO:** Then they brought the detector down to sea level, in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and counted again.
*VISUAL: aerial of a river city / Cambridge-style campus (PX)*

**VO:** The math looked simple. Given how long a muon lives, and how long the trip down the mountain takes, then for every 568 muons at the top, only about 27 should have survived to the bottom.
*VISUAL: GFX — 568 dots falling, fading to 27*

**VO:** They counted 412.
*VISUAL: hold on black, then GFX 412 lights up*

**VO:** Fifteen times more than expected.

**VO:** The only explanation that fits is Einstein's. For a particle moving at more than ninety-nine percent of the speed of light, time runs slow. From our point of view, the muon's internal clock is barely ticking. A trip that should take several of its lifetimes takes only a fraction of one.
*VISUAL: 3D light-streak particle racing down past a mountain ridge (3D)*

**VO:** The muons weren't breaking the rules. They were living in a different time than we are.

**VO:** But muons are exotic — tiny, fast, far away. What about something ordinary? Like a clock… on a plane.
*VISUAL: airport window, plane at gate at dusk (PX)*

## 2. The Airplanes (3:15–5:45)

**VO:** In October 1971, a physicist named Joseph Hafele and an astronomer from the U.S. Naval Observatory, Richard Keating, did something that sounds like a joke.
*VISUAL: vintage-graded airport terminal (PX), film grain*

**VO:** They bought plane tickets for atomic clocks.
*VISUAL: airplane cabin, empty seat (PX)*

**VO:** Four cesium-beam clocks — among the most precise timekeepers of the era — flew on ordinary commercial flights, all the way around the world. First heading east. Then heading west.
*VISUAL: 3D/GFX globe with eastward then westward flight paths; plane above clouds (PX)*

**VO:** Back in Washington, the Naval Observatory's clocks stayed on the ground, ticking as usual.
*VISUAL: Washington DC aerial (PX)*

**VO:** Einstein's theories predicted two effects pulling in opposite directions. Speed slows time down. Height speeds it up — the weaker the gravity, the faster a clock ticks. And because the Earth itself is spinning, flying east or west changes how fast the clocks are really moving.
*VISUAL: GFX two dials — "speed" and "height"; Earth rotation from orbit (NASA)*

**VO:** When the clocks came home, they no longer agreed with the ones that stayed behind.
*VISUAL: landing at night (PX)*

**VO:** The eastbound clocks had lost about 59 billionths of a second. The westbound clocks had gained about 273.
*VISUAL: GFX −59 ns / +273 ns*

**VO:** The predictions? A loss of about 40, and a gain of about 275 — agreement within the uncertainties of the experiment.

**VO:** Two trips around the planet. A few hundred billionths of a second. And Einstein was right again.

**VO:** Today, this isn't just a curiosity. GPS satellites orbit about twenty thousand kilometers up, and their clocks run faster than ours by about thirty-eight millionths of a second every day. Leave that uncorrected, and your phone's map would drift by kilometers within a single day.
*VISUAL: GPS satellite animation (NASA) → phone map in a car at night (PX)*

**VO:** But a plane still flies ten kilometers high. How small can the difference get — and still be measured?
*VISUAL: plane shrinking into clouds (PX)*

## 3. Two Stair Steps (5:45–8:00)

**VO:** In 2010, physicists at the National Institute of Standards and Technology, in Boulder, Colorado, had built something far better than a cesium clock.
*VISUAL: Boulder / Flatirons aerial (PX)*

**VO:** Their clocks ticked using a single aluminum ion — one electrically charged atom, held in place by electric fields and read out with lasers. The best of them kept time to within one second in about 3.7 billion years.
*VISUAL: NIST photo of the aluminum ion clock (NIST, credit) → 3D single glowing ion in a trap*

**VO:** So they took two of these clocks, and asked a very small question.
*VISUAL: 3D — two identical clocks side by side*

**VO:** They raised one of them by 33 centimeters. About a foot. Roughly two steps on a staircase.
*VISUAL: 3D — one clock rises; staircase footage (PX)*

**VO:** And the higher clock ran faster.
*VISUAL: 3D — upper clock's glow pulses faster*

**VO:** Not by much. Over a 79-year lifetime, that difference adds up to about 90 billionths of a second.
*VISUAL: time-lapse of a life — child to elderly hands (PX)*

**VO:** But it's measurable. Every time you climb the stairs, you age a tiny bit faster. Every time you lie down, your head and your feet fall back in step.
*VISUAL: person running up stairs (PX) → person lying in bed, morning light (PX)*

**VO:** They tested the other half of Einstein's idea too. They set one ion moving back and forth at just a few meters per second — about the speed of a jog — and that clock ticked slower.
*VISUAL: runner at dawn, slow motion (PX)*

**VO:** Time bends with height. Time bends with speed. Even at human scale.

**VO:** But thirty-three centimeters still wasn't the limit.

## 4. One Millimeter (8:00–10:15)

**VO:** In 2022, a team led by physicist Jun Ye at JILA — a joint institute of NIST and the University of Colorado Boulder — pushed the question to its extreme.
*VISUAL: laser lab, optics table, beams (PX) → NIST photo of the JILA clock (NIST, credit)*

**VO:** They didn't compare two clocks. They used one cloud.
*VISUAL: 3D atom_cell, push-in to the glowing cloud*

**VO:** About a hundred thousand strontium atoms, trapped in a vacuum chamber and cooled to a tiny fraction of a degree above absolute zero. Laser light stacks them into thin layers — like a stack of pancakes made of light — and every atom in the stack becomes a clock.
*VISUAL: 3D — cloud splits into horizontal glowing layers*

**VO:** The whole stack is about a millimeter tall. Roughly the width of a sharp pencil tip.
*VISUAL: macro pencil tip (PX)*

**VO:** Then they compared the ticking at the top of the cloud with the ticking at the bottom.
*VISUAL: 3D — top layers tinted slightly faster pulse*

**VO:** And the top was ticking faster.

**VO:** Across one millimeter, the difference in rate is about one part in ten billion billion. To see it, the team compared frequencies with an uncertainty of just 7.6 parts in a thousand billion billion. If a clock were that precise, it would drift less than a hundredth of a second over the entire age of the universe.
*VISUAL: GFX 10⁻¹⁹ ; galaxy/deep field (NASA/ESA)*

**VO:** And they didn't just catch the effect once. They saw it as a smooth gradient — tick rates changing steadily from the bottom of the cloud to the top — exactly as Einstein's general relativity predicts.
*VISUAL: 3D layers with gradient color bottom→top*

**VO:** Our best clocks are now so sensitive that they can feel time change across something smaller than a grain of sand.

## 5. Finale (10:15–11:00)

**VO:** So here is where four experiments leave us.
*VISUAL: night sky time-lapse (PX)*

**VO:** There is no single time. No master clock ticking for the whole universe. Every place has its own time — a little faster where gravity is weaker, a little slower where you're moving faster.
*VISUAL: city aerial at night; Earth from orbit (NASA)*

**VO:** A particle racing down a mountain. Clocks with plane tickets. Two stair steps. A cloud one millimeter tall.
*VISUAL: rapid recap of each hero shot*

**VO:** Time isn't the background of the universe. It's part of the landscape — and it bends.
*VISUAL: 3D atom_cell wide shot*

**VO:** And right now, as you watch this, the top of your head is getting older than your feet.
*VISUAL: slow tilt from face down to feet (PX), mirror of the opening*

**VO:** Not by much. But it is.
*VISUAL: cut to black → channel end card*
