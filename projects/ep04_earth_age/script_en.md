# How Do We Know How Old the Earth Is?

Target: ~8 min narration (~1,250 words).
Format per block: **VO** → *VISUAL*.

Legend: `PX` Pexels · `3D` our Blender render · `GFX` motion graphics

---

## 0. Cold open (0:00–0:50)

**VO:** There is no rock on Earth as old as the Earth. The first ones were melted, buried and remade billions of years ago. So how do we know the planet is four and a half billion years old?
*VISUAL: PX — Earth from orbit, volcano, lava; GFX 4.54 billion years*

**VO:** The answer came from a rock that fell from the sky — and from a chemist who could not measure it, because everything he touched was poisoned with lead. The air. The water. The dust on his own hands.
*VISUAL: PX — traffic, exhaust, a hand, dust in a light beam*

**VO:** To find the age of the Earth, he had to build the cleanest room on the planet. And to explain the lead, he had to take on one of the biggest industries in the world.
*VISUAL: PX cleanroom; 3D — the meteorite fragment*

**VO:** He won both fights.
*VISUAL: PX — petrol pump, city haze*

**VO:** This is how we learned how old the Earth is.
*VISUAL: title card*

## I. Too young (0:50–2:30)

**VO:** The first scientific answer came in 1862, from the most famous physicist in Britain.
*VISUAL: PX — old book pages, candlelit study*

**VO:** William Thomson — later Lord Kelvin — assumed the Earth had started as a ball of molten rock and had been cooling ever since. Measure how fast heat leaks out of the ground today, and you can run the clock backwards.
*VISUAL: PX — lava cooling to black crust; 3D — the molten sphere*

**VO:** His answer: twenty to four hundred million years. Later he narrowed it to twenty to forty. He was wrong by a factor of a hundred — and almost nobody could argue with him.
*VISUAL: GFX — 20–400 million years → 20–40*

**VO:** Geologists hated it. Their layers of rock, their canyons cut grain by grain, seemed to need far longer. Darwin hated it more: twenty million years was nowhere near enough time for evolution.
*VISUAL: PX — Grand Canyon strata, river cutting rock*

**VO:** Others tried different clocks. The Irish physicist John Joly asked how long the rivers would need to carry all the salt that is now in the sea. His answer, in 1899: about ninety million years.
*VISUAL: PX — waves, salt crystals, river mouth*

**VO:** Every clock gave a different answer. And all of them were wrong for the same reason: each assumed a process that had run steadily, and nothing had.
*VISUAL: PX — hourglass, sand running*

---

## II. A clock inside the rock (2:30–4:00)

**VO:** The way out came from somewhere nobody was looking. In 1896, Henri Becquerel found that uranium gives off radiation by itself. Within a few years, Ernest Rutherford showed what that meant: atoms were falling apart.
*VISUAL: PX — glowing mineral, Geiger counter, lab glassware*

**VO:** That broke Kelvin's calculation twice. Radioactive decay heats the Earth from inside, so it cools far more slowly than he assumed. And it gave geologists a new kind of clock.
*VISUAL: 3D — the zircon crystal, slowly turning*

**VO:** Uranium decays, through a long chain of steps, into lead, at a rate that never changes — not with heat, not with pressure, not with time. Half of any uranium-238 turns into lead in four and a half billion years.
*VISUAL: 3D — atoms in the crystal switching colour; GFX half-life 4.47 billion years*

**VO:** So if a crystal locked in uranium when it formed, and no lead, then the lead inside it now is a stopwatch. Count the atoms, and you know how long it has been running.
*VISUAL: 3D — the crystal filling with lead atoms*

**VO:** In 1907, the American chemist Bertram Boltwood tried it on real rocks and got ages of up to two billion years. By the 1940s, the oldest rocks pointed to about three billion. The Earth had to be older still — but how much older, no rock on Earth could say.
*VISUAL: PX — rock outcrops, ancient cliffs; GFX 2.2 billion → 3 billion*

---

## III. The poisoned laboratory (4:00–5:40)

**VO:** Remember: no rock on Earth goes back to the beginning. To find the starting line, you need material that formed with the Earth and was never touched again.
*VISUAL: PX — magma, subduction, waves on rocks*

**VO:** Meteorites are exactly that: leftovers from the same cloud that built the planets. In 1948, a young chemist in Chicago named Clair Patterson was given the job of measuring the lead inside them.
*VISUAL: 3D — the meteorite fragment; PX — microscope, lab bench*

**VO:** It should have taken months. It took seven years. Every time he measured, he found far more lead than could possibly be there. The extra lead was coming from the laboratory itself — from the air, the glassware, the dust, the paint.
*VISUAL: PX — dust in a sunbeam, old lab, pipettes*

**VO:** So Patterson built one of the first clean rooms in science. Filtered air. Surfaces scrubbed with acid. Chemicals distilled again and again. Anyone entering had to be stripped of lead, down to the dust on their skin.
*VISUAL: PX — cleanroom, gowned figures, acid washing glass*

**VO:** In 1953, working at Argonne National Laboratory, he finally measured clean lead from a meteorite that had struck Arizona fifty thousand years before. His result: four and a half billion years. Published in 1956: four point five five, give or take seventy million.
*VISUAL: PX — Meteor Crater, desert; GFX 4.55 ± 0.07 billion years*

**VO:** Seventy years later, the accepted age is four point five four billion years. His number has barely moved.
*VISUAL: GFX — 4.55 → 4.54*

---

## IV. Where the lead came from (5:40–7:20)

**VO:** But Patterson could not stop thinking about the contamination. Where did all that lead in the air come from?
*VISUAL: PX — city haze, streetlights*

**VO:** He started measuring lead everywhere — in seawater, in ancient sediments, in layers of ice in Greenland. The pattern was brutal. Old ice held almost none. Recent ice held many times more, and the steepest rise had come in just the last few decades.
*VISUAL: PX — Greenland ice, ice core, snow*

**VO:** 1923 was the year tetraethyl lead was first sold as a petrol additive. It stopped engines knocking. And every car on Earth had been spraying lead into the air ever since.
*VISUAL: PX — old cars, petrol pump, exhaust*

**VO:** In 1965 Patterson published it: the lead in modern people was far above natural levels. The fuel industry pushed back hard. His funding was cut, and he was left off a national committee on lead pollution.
*VISUAL: PX — papers, typewriter, courtroom-like hall*

**VO:** He kept going. In 1970 America passed the Clean Air Act, and leaded petrol was phased out over the next twenty-five years. Lead in Americans' blood fell by more than three quarters.
*VISUAL: PX — traffic, clean sky, children playing*

**VO:** The last country on Earth stopped selling leaded petrol for cars in 2021.
*VISUAL: PX — sunrise over a highway*

---

## V. The number (7:20–8:10)

**VO:** Today the oldest pieces of the solar system are dated to four billion five hundred and sixty-seven million years. The oldest crystal found on Earth, a zircon from Western Australia, is four point four billion years old.
*VISUAL: 3D — zircon; PX — Australian outback; GFX 4.567 · 4.4*

**VO:** And the Earth itself: four point five four billion, give or take about one per cent — read from the lead inside a rock that fell from the sky.
*VISUAL: 3D — the meteorite, final slow turn*

**VO:** Clair Patterson found the age of the world by learning to see lead. And then he got the lead out of it.
*VISUAL: PX — Earth from orbit*

**VO:** The last digit is still being argued over. The first ones have not moved since 1956.
*VISUAL: end card*
