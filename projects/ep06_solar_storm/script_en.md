# The Sun Already Tried to Switch Off the Internet

Target: ~5 min narration. Lines kept short: one idea each, so the narration can breathe and the cut can move.
Format per block: **VO** → *VISUAL*.

---

## 0. Cold open (0:00–0:45)

**VO(excited):** At one in the morning, gold miners in the Rocky Mountains got up and started making breakfast.
*VISUAL: mountain night, campfire, sky glowing red — fast*

**VO(confident):** The sky was so bright, they thought the Sun had risen.
*VISUAL: aurora, red and green, over mountains*

**VO(excited):** That same night, telegraph wires threw sparks. Operators got electric shocks. In some offices, the paper caught fire.
*VISUAL: sparks, old wires, flame — fast*

**VO(confident):** It was September 1859. And the Sun had just hit the Earth with the biggest storm we have ever recorded.
*VISUAL: the Sun, a flare erupting*

**VO:** In 2012, it fired another one just as big. It missed us by about a week.
*VISUAL: Earth from orbit, the Sun behind*

**VO(confident):** This is the story of the storm that already happened — and what it would do to a world that now runs on electricity.
*VISUAL: city lights from orbit; title over footage*

---

## I. Two beads of light (0:45–1:40)

**VO:** On the morning of the first of September, an English astronomer named Richard Carrington was drawing sunspots.
*VISUAL: old telescope, paper, pencil*

**VO:** He pointed his telescope at the Sun and projected its image onto a sheet of paper — the safe way to look at it.
*VISUAL: projected Sun disc, hand sketching*

**VO(excited):** At eleven eighteen, two beads of blinding white light burst out of a group of sunspots.
*VISUAL: the Sun, a flare — close*

**VO:** He ran to find a witness. By the time he got back, a minute later, the light was already fading. Five minutes — and it was gone.
*VISUAL: corridor, door, clock*

**VO(confident):** Carrington had seen the first solar flare in history.
*VISUAL: the Sun, slow push in*

**VO:** What he couldn't see was the cloud it launched: billions of tonnes of magnetised gas, flying towards the Earth.
*VISUAL: plasma, space*

**VO(excited):** Storms like that usually take two or three days to cross. This one took seventeen and a half hours.
*VISUAL: space timelapse, Earth approaching*

---

## II. The night the sky caught fire (1:40–2:40)

**VO:** When it hit, the Earth's magnetic field shook like a bell.
*VISUAL: Earth, magnetic field lines*

**VO:** Auroras — the northern lights — were seen over Cuba, over Jamaica, over Hawaii.
*VISUAL: aurora over palm trees / sea*

**VO(cheerful):** In the north-eastern United States, people read their newspapers outside, at midnight, by the light of the sky.
*VISUAL: aurora over a town, a person reading*

**VO:** But the strangest thing happened on the wires.
*VISUAL: telegraph poles at dusk*

**VO:** The storm pushed electric currents into every long metal line on Earth. And in 1859, the longest metal lines were telegraph wires.
*VISUAL: wires stretching to the horizon*

**VO(cheerful):** Between Boston and Portland, the operators unplugged their batteries completely — and kept sending messages for two hours, powered by the sky.
*VISUAL: Morse key, hands tapping*

**VO(confident):** In 1859 that was a curiosity. Today, we have wrapped the whole planet in metal lines.
*VISUAL: power lines, a grid at night from above*

---

## III. What a storm does now (2:40–3:45)

**VO:** We don't have to guess. Smaller storms have already shown us.
*VISUAL: the Sun, flare*

**VO(excited):** In March 1989, a storm hit Quebec. The power grid collapsed in about ninety seconds. Six million people lost electricity.
*VISUAL: power station, lights going out, snowy city*

**VO:** In 1972, a storm set off dozens of American sea mines off the coast of Vietnam. Nobody touched them. The magnetic field did.
*VISUAL: ocean, grey sea, warship*

**VO(frustrated):** In 2022, SpaceX launched forty-nine Starlink satellites into a minor storm. It puffed up the atmosphere, and dragged thirty-eight of them back down to burn.
*VISUAL: rocket launch, satellite, re-entry streak*

**VO(cheerful):** And in May 2024, in the middle of planting season, farm tractors across the American Midwest lost their GPS and stopped steering themselves.
*VISUAL: tractor, fields, GPS screen*

**VO:** None of these were Carrington-sized. Not even close.
*VISUAL: the Sun, big*

---

## IV. The miss (3:45–4:30)

**VO:** On the twenty-third of July 2012, the Sun fired a cloud that was.
*VISUAL: the Sun, an eruption*

**VO:** A NASA spacecraft called STEREO happened to be in its path, and measured it. It was as fast as 1859. Maybe faster.
*VISUAL: satellite in space*

**VO(confident):** It crossed the Earth's orbit where we had been about a week earlier.
*VISUAL: Earth orbiting, empty space where it was*

**VO(sad):** A report for the US National Academies estimated what a direct hit could cost: up to two trillion dollars in the first year — and four to ten years to recover.
*VISUAL: dark city, candles, empty streets*

**VO:** The weakest point is the giant transformers that run the grid. Burn enough of them, and there are no spares. Each one is built to order, and takes a year or more to make.
*VISUAL: substation, transformer, factory*

---

## V. The watchers (4:30–5:05)

**VO:** Today, we're not drawing sunspots on paper.
*VISUAL: observatory, telescope*

**VO:** Spacecraft watch the Sun all day, every day. When a cloud leaves it, we know within hours — and get one to three days' warning.
*VISUAL: satellite, the Sun in extreme UV*

**VO(confident):** And a probe parked a million and a half kilometres towards the Sun gives us the final alarm: somewhere between fifteen minutes and an hour.
*VISUAL: deep space, Earth small*

**VO:** Enough to put satellites to sleep, reroute flights away from the poles, and ease the load on the grid.
*VISUAL: control room, airplane, power lines*

---

## VI. The last digit (5:05–5:40)

**VO:** So how likely is another Carrington?
*VISUAL: the Sun, slow*

**VO:** One study put it at about twelve percent per decade. Later ones say closer to one or two.
*VISUAL: aurora*

**VO(confident):** Nobody knows the last digit. We only know it isn't zero.
*VISUAL: aurora over a city*

**VO:** Somewhere on the Sun, the next one is already waiting. This time, we'll see it coming.
*VISUAL: the Sun, end card*
